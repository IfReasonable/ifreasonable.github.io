AI is steadily lowering the barriers to performing many specialized tasks. From software development and data analysis to content creation, more work that once required specialists can now be done at lower cost with AI. People often measure this change through productivity gains in individual tasks: how much faster they can write code, for example, or how much less time it takes to produce a report. But when AI begins to generalize across domains and tasks, it changes more than the efficiency of individual steps. It also changes how those steps are coordinated and the economic foundations of traditional specialization.

The division of labor in modern society is fundamentally an economic choice shaped by constraints on capabilities and costs. In software development, for example, a complete product typically involves specialists in frontend development, backend development, testing, and operations. These tasks require different knowledge and tools, and it is costly for one person to master and perform all of them. Specialization and teamwork can therefore improve overall efficiency. Yet this organizational model rests on an implicit condition: **the efficiency gains from specialization must be sufficient to cover the costs of communication, coordination, and handoffs between people.** As general-purpose language models become capable of handling different tasks and connecting to external systems through tools, work that once required several people may become feasible for fewer people. When the cost of working across domains falls, the economic rationale for existing divisions of labor needs to be reassessed.

This change also affects supply and demand in the labor market. On the demand side, existing teams can now use AI to handle many standardized tasks that previously required hiring specialists, reducing the need to purchase isolated professional capabilities. On the supply side, competitive advantages built around a particular skill may weaken as that capability becomes cheaper to acquire. When more people can use AI to reach similar levels of execution, the scarcity and bargaining power of individual skills may come under pressure. This raises a more important question: **when individual capabilities become less scarce, how should people expand the value they can create?**

## 1. Breadth First, with Sufficient Depth

A complete business process usually consists of many connected steps, from understanding requirements and designing a solution to delivery and feedback. Together, these form a long value chain. An individual may not be able to cover the entire business cycle, but can gradually expand the portion they can handle independently. Suppose a value chain has ten steps. Someone who could previously handle only one might, with AI, be able to take on three, five, or more consecutive steps. Even without covering the entire process, they have developed a form of partial end-to-end capability. Its value comes from both completing more tasks and reducing the communication, coordination, and handoff costs that previously arose between people.

This offers a way to reconsider two directions of personal development: breadth and depth. Greater depth usually improves the efficiency, quality, or cost advantage of a particular step—for example, reducing ten hours of work to eight or delivering higher quality at the same cost. Expanding breadth has a different payoff structure. When one person can carry out several consecutive steps, some of the costs of communicating requirements, handing off tasks, coordinating interfaces, and redoing work can be reduced. As AI improves at standardized tasks, competition over efficiency in individual steps may intensify. Savings from integrating adjacent steps offer another way for individuals to build a competitive advantage.

Therefore, **as AI continues to lower the barriers to acquiring professional capabilities, prioritizing breadth is a career development strategy worth considering.** This does not mean that a wider range of capabilities is always better. If covering more steps significantly reduces the quality of a critical deliverable, the additional costs of verification, rework, and failure may outweigh the coordination costs saved. For standardized tasks with easily verifiable results, AI and an appropriate level of professional knowledge may be enough to meet business requirements. More complex tasks, especially those with costly errors, require deeper expertise to ensure reliability. The expansion of breadth must therefore be constrained by sufficient depth, with the required depth depending on the quality standards and risks of the specific setting.

A more reasonable strategy is **breadth first, with sufficient depth.** While maintaining reliable delivery, first explore adjacent steps where taking on more responsibility can reduce coordination costs. When a weakness in one step threatens overall quality, deepen the relevant expertise. There is no universal definition of “sufficient,” and it does not simply mean meeting the bare minimum. It requires judging, in a particular business context, whether the marginal returns from greater depth have fallen below those available from expanding breadth. This turns the choice between breadth and depth from an abstract career preference into a resource allocation problem that can be evaluated economically.

## 2. The Real Challenge of End-to-End Systems: From Tool Integration to Global Optimization

The value of expanding breadth is relatively easy to understand for general-purpose tasks. In highly specialized domains, the problem is often more complex. Many industries have accumulated substantial bodies of knowledge, mathematical models, simulation tools, and optimization algorithms over time. These capabilities may not be adequately represented in the training of general-purpose models, and some tools are used only within particular professional communities. Building a useful end-to-end system in these domains therefore usually requires combining general-purpose models, domain knowledge, specialized models, and external tools to perform complex tasks together.

Connecting tools, however, is only the starting point of systems integration. Different tools may have been designed in different contexts to solve different problems. Their data structures, timescales, core assumptions, and optimization objectives may not align. A forecasting model, for example, may rely on one set of assumptions about a system, while a downstream optimizer operates under a different set of constraints. Simply connecting outputs to inputs may produce a pipeline that appears complete but is unreliable in practice. Resolving these problems requires understanding the underlying principles, scope, and key assumptions of each tool. It may also require redesigning intermediate layers, adjusting interfaces, or modifying the tools themselves so that they work toward common objectives under compatible constraints. This illustrates the role of depth in integration: a system builder need not master every detail of every tool, but must understand the mechanisms that can affect the correctness of the whole system.

Even when every tool works well in isolation, their combination may not produce a good overall outcome. A forecasting model may have high average accuracy, yet cause substantial decision errors if its mistakes occur in regions to which the downstream optimizer is particularly sensitive. Local optima pursued by separate modules may also conflict with the overall business objective. An end-to-end system therefore needs a global evaluation and feedback mechanism that reflects final business value. It must be able not only to perform tasks, but also to determine whether they actually achieved their objectives, where deviations arose, and how earlier steps should be adjusted. **Establishing common objectives, aligning assumptions, evaluating outcomes, and closing feedback loops are important technical capabilities in end-to-end integration.** As AI makes individual tasks easier, these system-level capabilities may become a new source of value.

Systems integration nevertheless carries costs that cannot be ignored: verification and failure risk. Tasks performed by multiple experts involve communication and handoff costs, but collaboration can also provide a form of checking. Integrating these steps into an automated system may greatly improve execution efficiency, while also allowing errors in early stages to propagate throughout the toolchain and cause larger losses. Evaluating the economics of an end-to-end system requires accounting for execution, coordination, verification, and risk together:

**Total cost = execution cost + coordination cost + verification cost + expected failure loss**

Expected failure loss can be approximated as the probability of failure multiplied by the loss from a single failure. For an individual, whether to use AI to take on a task previously delegated to another professional depends on whether doing it independently lowers the total cost. Organizations can make a similar comparison when deciding whether to automate a step with AI. If necessary quality and safety constraints cannot be met, a lower expected cost alone does not justify replacing human work. **The effective boundary of end-to-end capability ultimately depends on whether further expansion can create greater overall net value while meeting reliability requirements.**

This boundary is dynamic. A step may be unsuitable for automation today because verification is expensive and failure risk is high. As models become more reliable, specialized tools improve, and verification systems mature, it may become economically viable. Conversely, higher quality and risk standards may require reintroducing specialization into tasks that one person or system could previously handle. Both personal capability development and organizational automation therefore require ongoing assessment of the overall returns from different steps, with boundaries adjusted as technology and costs change. This leads naturally to another question: **with limited resources, should the next investment go toward greater breadth, deeper expertise, or better verification?**

## 3. Shifting Value Bottlenecks: Where Should the Next Investment Go?

Time, money, and attention are limited for both individuals and organizations. Greater technical depth can improve the efficiency and reliability of existing steps. Expanding breadth can reduce coordination costs between steps. Better verification can reduce failure risk and may even make previously unsuitable steps viable for automation. All these directions can be valuable. The key is to compare the marginal returns from equivalent investments while considering their long-term effects. A simplified relationship is: **incremental net value = additional benefits + costs saved − additional investment − additional risk-related losses.** Long-term investments also require considering the time value of future returns and how current capabilities affect later opportunities. Deepening one skill may quickly increase short-term income, while another capability with little immediate return may make it possible to connect several additional business steps later.

Assessing long-term value raises a deeper question: **if future AI can not only perform individual tasks but also independently build, integrate, and verify complex end-to-end systems, will the integration capabilities emphasized today also lose their scarcity?** This is entirely possible. Any capability that can be standardized and automated may become cheaper to acquire as technology matures. Integration that requires extensive expertise and engineering experience today may become straightforward in the future. When technical implementation is no longer the main obstacle, competitive advantage may shift to other areas, such as product design, user experience, customer relationships, trust, distribution, and responsibility for risk.

This reflects the ongoing **migration of bottlenecks in value creation** as technology develops. When technical capabilities are inadequate, those who overcome technical limitations may capture more opportunities to create value. As technology matures, new product forms become viable, shifting competition toward user needs, product experience, and commercialization. As those applications expand, existing technologies encounter new limitations, creating demand for another round of technical advances. Technology and products develop in a spiral: technical progress enables product innovation, product innovation realizes the commercial value of technology, and new product demands drive further technical development.

The development of large language models provides an example. Early research focused more heavily on model architectures, training methods, and basic capabilities. Once models reached a certain level of capability, conversational products substantially lowered the barriers for ordinary users and encouraged widespread adoption. As code generation, tool use, and complex task execution improved, AI coding tools and agent products began to offer additional value. Product opportunities depend on underlying technical capabilities crossing a threshold. Turning those capabilities into widely accepted products also requires sound judgments about user needs and interaction design. Following this logic, investment in technology and products must adapt as bottlenecks shift; neither direction is likely to remain the right choice forever.

Long-term competitiveness therefore also depends on identifying current value bottlenecks and anticipating the next stage of change. The best investment today may be a critical technology. A few months later, it may be systems integration, followed by product design or market development. **Rather than committing indefinitely to a fixed direction of capability development, it is better to keep identifying the factors most likely to constrain value creation next and develop the relevant capabilities in advance.** This introduces a practical difficulty: how far will technology advance, and how will market demand change? Without reliable evidence, resource allocation can become little more than subjective speculation about the future.

## 4. Decisions Under Uncertainty: Information, Forecasting, and Low-Cost Experiments

Predicting changes in technology and markets is difficult. Over the next three months, how much will the success rate of language models improve on a particular task? Is a technology close to crossing the reliability threshold required for commercialization? When will businesses begin paying for that capability? Answers to these questions directly affect career choices and organizational investment. Although the future cannot be predicted precisely, forecast quality can improve through continuous information gathering, observation of technical and market signals, and evaluation of earlier judgments against subsequent outcomes.

As technology moves from research to commercialization, it usually leaves different observable signals. Research papers and technical experiments offer early indications of capability breakthroughs. Open-source communities and tool ecosystems reflect usability and maturity. Corporate pilots and early products provide further evidence of the potential to solve real problems. As commercialization progresses, hiring demand, salaries, project quotes, and procurement budgets begin to reveal willingness to pay. Customer retention, project profits, and repeat purchasing offer more direct evidence of whether commercial value has been demonstrated. Information sources can be evaluated along two dimensions: **predictive accuracy and signal lead time.** Earlier signals may offer greater first-mover opportunities but usually involve more uncertainty. Information closer to actual market transactions often provides more direct commercial evidence, but may arrive after others have already identified part of the opportunity.

Access to information differs across individuals and industries. Some people have easier access to frontier research and developer communities. Others learn about real demand through customer conversations, project quotes, friends' industry experience, or the hiring market. The analytical logic is similar: observe whether a technical capability is approaching the threshold for commercialization, whether demand and willingness to pay are emerging, and whether the supply of that capability is expanding quickly. Tracking these signals over time and comparing them with later outcomes can help build more reliable forecasting models. Forecast accuracy, however, does not eliminate decision risk. Even when a direction has a high probability of success, deciding whether to invest immediately requires considering costs, potential returns, and the value of waiting.

This involves a tradeoff between positioning early and waiting for validation. Early investment may secure a first-mover advantage but exposes the investor to greater uncertainty. Waiting provides more information and can reduce the probability of a mistaken judgment, but may sacrifice some opportunities. Economically, waiting has option value because it preserves the right to decide later using new information. The decision to wait requires comparing the improvement in decision quality from additional information with the opportunities lost during the delay. AI development offers a more flexible way to approach this problem: **when exploring a new direction is cheap enough, small experiments can provide information and preserve future options, allowing investment to expand only when results justify it.**

For example, a brand marketing manager at a consumer goods company may want to assess whether to expand from executing marketing tasks to managing complete campaigns. They could choose a small project within their current role and use AI to cover some tasks that previously required coordination with copywriters, designers, and data analysts. They could then track execution time, communication costs, verification effort, and final marketing outcomes. If the experiment improves overall delivery efficiency, there is a reason to gradually expand their independent responsibilities. If quality falls in particular steps or verification costs become excessive, they can address the capability gaps or retain the existing division of labor. Such experiments also let people assess the long-term merits of a career direction at low cost, without first assuming the risks of a complete transition.

The same logic applies to organizations. Before it is clear whether an AI technology has commercial value, an organization can pilot it in a setting with manageable risks and verifiable outcomes. Real data on costs, benefits, and reliability can then inform decisions about broader deployment. As evidence accumulates, resource allocation that once depended on subjective judgment can gradually become a dynamic, data-informed decision process. Investment can increase in directions with high long-term returns supported by continuing validation. Directions whose key assumptions have been disproven, whose risks are excessive, or whose economic advantages are weak should be adjusted promptly. This strategy combines exploration of future opportunities with control over failure costs, preserving flexibility in a rapidly changing technological environment.

## Conclusion

AI's generalization capabilities are changing the cost structure of specialization and encouraging individuals and organizations to reconsider the scope of value they can deliver. As individual capabilities become cheaper to acquire, covering a longer stretch of the value chain may become an important competitive strategy. As that scope expands, professional depth, verification costs, and failure risks jointly determine its economic boundaries. Over longer timescales, technical progress continues to change these constraints, shifting value bottlenecks among technical capabilities, systems integration, product design, and business resources.

Career development and organizational investment can thus be understood as the same kind of dynamic resource allocation problem: within limited time, capital, and capabilities, continually identify the next action with the greatest long-term expected net value. Breadth and depth, technology and products, early positioning and waiting for validation all require choices based on current conditions, followed by adjustment as new information and experimental results emerge.

**A capability worth developing throughout the AI era is the ability to understand the full value chain, identify present and future constraints, and continually expand the scope of value one can reliably deliver while keeping costs and risks manageable.** This requires sensitivity to technology, attention to markets, and a willingness to test judgments through action. As specific skills and tools become easier to acquire, understanding how value is created, deciding where to invest next, and turning those judgments into reliable delivery will be important foundations of long-term competitiveness.
